Question 2

The Uniform-Pareto
The goal of this problem is to continue getting more practice calculating the posterior distribution.

Suppose \(a < x < b.\) Consider the notation \(I_{(a,b)}(x),\) where \(I\) denotes the indicator function. We define \(I_{(a,b)}(x)\) to be the following:

\[ I_{(a,b)}(x)= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if $a < x < b$,} \\ 0 &\text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Let X be a random variable and let x be an observed value. Let

\[ \begin{aligned} X=x \mid \theta &\sim \text{Uniform}(0,\theta)\\ \theta &\sim \text{Pareto}(\alpha,\beta), \end{aligned} \]

where \(p(\theta) = \dfrac{\alpha\beta^\alpha}{\theta^{\alpha+1}}I_{(\beta,\infty)}(\theta).\) Write out the likelihood \(p(X=x\mid \theta).\)

Then calculate the posterior distribution of \(\theta|X=x.\)

\(\textbf{Hint 1}\): To set up this problem, do the following:

\[p(\theta \mid x) \propto \frac{1}{\theta} I_{(0,\theta)}(x) \times \frac{\alpha \beta^{\alpha}} {\theta^{\alpha + 1}} I_{(\beta, \infty)}(\theta).\]

\(\textbf{Hint 2}\): You cannot drop the indicators. You must write the indicators as functions of \(\theta\) since this is the random variable.

\(\textbf{Hint 3}\): It will end up being an updated Pareto.

________

Proof. Consider the notation \(I_{(a,b)}(x),\) where \(I\) denotes the indicator function. We define \(I_{(a,b)}(x)\) to be the following:

\[ I_{(a,b)}(x)= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if $a < x < b$,} \\ 0 &\text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Let X be a random variable and let x be an observed value. Let

\[ \begin{aligned} X=x \mid \theta &\sim \text{Uniform}(0,\theta)\\ \theta &\sim \text{Pareto}(\alpha,\beta), \end{aligned} \]

where \(p(\theta) = \dfrac{\alpha\beta^\alpha}{\theta^{\alpha+1}}I_{(\beta,\infty)}(\theta).\) The likelihood \(p(X=x\mid \theta)\) may be written as

\[ p(X=x\mid \theta) = \left( \frac {1}{\theta - 0} \right)^n * \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x) = \left( \frac {1}{\theta} \right)^n * \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x). \]

We will calculate the posterior distribution of \(\theta|X=x.\) By Bayes’ Theorem, observe

\[ p(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x, \theta)}{p(x)} = \frac{p(x \mid \theta) * p(\theta)}{p(x)} \propto p(x \mid \theta) * p(\theta). \]

when we absorb terms that do not depend on \(\theta\) ( i.e., \(p(x)\)) into the constant of proportionality. Substituting in our likelihood and prior gives

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid \theta) *p(\theta) \\&= \left( \left( \frac {1}{\theta} \right)^n * \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x) \right) * \left( \dfrac{\alpha\beta^\alpha}{\theta^{\alpha+1}}I_{(\beta,\infty)}(\theta) \right) \\ &\propto \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x) \frac{1}{\theta^{(\alpha+n)+1}}I_{(\beta,\infty)}(\theta) . \end{aligned} \]

When we absorb terms not depending on \(\theta\) into the constant of proportionality. To simplify the indicator functions into 1 term, we must consider the region where both

\[ \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x) = 1 \]

and

\[ \mathbf{I}_{(\beta, \infty)}(\theta) = 1 \]

, which is true for \(\theta\) between the maximum value of itself and \(\beta\) and \(\infty\). Thus, we have

\[ \mathbf{I}_{(0, \theta)}(x) \frac{1}{\theta^{(\alpha+n)+1}}I_{(\beta,\infty)}(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta^{(\alpha+n)+1}}I_{(\max(\beta,x),\infty)}(\theta) \propto \text{Pareto}(\alpha' = \alpha+1, \beta' = \max(\beta,x),\infty). \]

Therefore, the posterior distribution of of \(\theta|X=x\) is and updated Pareto distribution,

\[ \theta\mid X=x \sim \text{Pareto}(\alpha' = \alpha+1, \beta' = \max(\beta,x),\infty). \]

Question 3

The Bayes estimator or Bayes rule
The goal of this problem is to practice a similar problem that we considered in Module 2, where we derived the Bayes rule under squared error loss and found the result was the posterior mean.

Part A

Find the Bayes estimator (or Bayes rule) when the loss function is \(L(\theta, \delta(x))~=~c~(\theta-\delta(x))^2,\) where \(c >0\) is a constant.

Proof. Suppose we have the loss function \(L(\theta, \delta(x))~=~c~(\theta-\delta(x))^2,\) where \(c >0\) is a constant. We will find the Bayes estimator, \(\hat{\delta}(x)\). Observe

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho(\theta,\delta(x)) &= E[L(\theta,\delta(x))|X] \\ &=E[c(\theta-\delta(x))^2), \delta(x)]\\ &=E[c(\theta^2-2\theta\delta(x)-\delta(x)^2)\mid X]\\ &=c(E[\theta^2\mid X]-2E[\theta\delta(x)\mid X]+E[\delta(x)^2\mid X]). \end{aligned} \]

To find the Bayes estimator, we take the derivative with respect to \(\delta(x)\) and solve for \(\hat{\delta}(x)\) when \(\frac{\partial \rho(\theta, \delta(x))}{\partial \delta(x)}=0\). Observe

\[ 0= \frac{\partial \rho(\theta, \delta(x))}{\partial \delta(x)}=c(-2E[\theta\mid X]+2E[\hat{\delta}(x)\mid X]). \]

Solving for \(\hat{\delta}(x)\) gives \(\hat{\delta}(x) = E[\theta \mid X]\). To check if the solution is unique, it must be true that \(\frac{\partial^2 \rho(\theta, \delta(x))}{\partial \delta(x)^2} >0.\) Here, we find

\[ \frac{\partial^2 \rho(\theta, \delta(x))}{\partial \delta(x)^2} = 2c>0. \]

since \(c>0\). Thus, our Bayes estimator is unique. QED
